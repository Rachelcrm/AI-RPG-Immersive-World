The Lantern of Saltmere

Chapter One: The Night the Light Went Out

For three hundred years the Great Lantern had burned atop the Drowned Bell Tower, and for three hundred years no ship had broken on the Teeth, the black reef that guards Saltmere Harbor. The lantern did not burn oil. It burned tidesalt, a pale blue crystal that grew only in the caves beneath the Kelp Moors, and it was tended by the Lamplighters' Guild, who had once numbered forty and now numbered one.

Her name was Ilsa Varn. She was seventy-one years old, she had a bad knee, and she climbed the four hundred steps of the tower every evening at dusk with a satchel of tidesalt on her back. The townsfolk called her the Lantern-Keeper, though rarely to her face. To her face they called her nothing at all, because Ilsa did not encourage conversation.

On the night of the autumn storm, she reached the top of the tower and found the lantern cold.

The great glass housing had not been broken. The brass vents were closed as she had left them. But the crystal bed was empty, scraped clean down to the iron, and someone had left a single gull feather on the rim of the bowl, tied with a red thread.

Ilsa knew that thread. Every dockhand in Saltmere knew it. It was the mark of the Brine Syndicate, the smugglers who ran the night trade out of Gullwing Market and who had wanted the Guild gone for as long as anyone could remember. A dark harbor meant ships forced to anchor outside the Teeth until morning, and a ship waiting outside the Teeth was a ship that needed a pilot boat, and the Syndicate owned every pilot boat in Saltmere.

She emptied her satchel into the bowl. It was enough for one night, perhaps two. The lantern caught with a sound like a breath drawn in, and blue light rolled out across the water just as the first merchant galley came around the point.

Below, on the quay, a boy was watching. His name was Tobin, he was eleven, and he ran the little rope ferry between the harbor and the Tidewater Archive on its rock across the inlet. He had seen a man in a grey oilskin coat come down the tower stairs an hour before the Lantern-Keeper went up. The man had been carrying a heavy sack and walking very carefully, the way people walk when what they carry is worth more than they are.

Tobin did not tell anyone that night. In Saltmere, people who saw things for the Syndicate's benefit were paid, and people who saw things to the Syndicate's harm were sometimes found under the pilings. But in the morning he climbed the four hundred steps himself, knocked on the iron door at the top, and told Ilsa Varn everything.

Chapter Two: Gullwing Market

Gullwing Market was built on old ships. When the herring fleet failed a century ago, the hulls were dragged up onto the mudflats and lashed together, and now three hundred stalls perched on their decks, joined by gangplanks and rope bridges. You could buy anything at Gullwing: smoked eel, Northern wool, charts of coastlines that did not exist, and, if you knew which stall had a red thread on its awning, tidesalt.

Hesper Quill sold lamps. Honest ones, mostly, brass and tin, and the occasional whale-oil lantern for the old captains who would not trust anything else. She was a broad woman with ink-stained fingers and a laugh that carried across three decks, and she had known Ilsa Varn for forty years, which made her the closest thing Ilsa had to a friend.

"Tidesalt's been cheap this week," Hesper said, when Ilsa and the boy came to her stall. "Too cheap. Six coins a handful, when it was twenty last spring. Somebody's dumping a great deal of it, and quickly."

"Who's buying?"

Hesper lowered her voice. "Corwin Dask. He's been buying everything that comes through, then selling it on to the Northern ships at triple the price. Says the Guild is finished and the harbor needs a modern light. Oil, maybe, or the new spark-lamps from the capital. Says he'll build it himself, if the council grants him the tower."

Corwin Dask was the Syndicate's broker, which meant he was the Syndicate's face: a soft-spoken man in a grey oilskin coat who gave money to the orphanage and attended every council meeting. Tobin went pale when Hesper described him.

Ilsa thought for a long while. Then she bought a cheap tin lantern, paid for it with a silver coin that was worth ten of them, and told Hesper to keep the change and keep her ears open.

That evening the council met in the Tidewater Archive, where Saltmere kept its charters and its tide records. Sister Maren of the Quiet Sisters, who kept the archive and had taken a vow to speak only when the tide was turning, sat at the end of the long table with her hands folded. Corwin Dask presented his proposal. The Guild, he said gently, had failed. One old woman could not keep a harbor safe. Last night proved it. He offered to purchase the Drowned Bell Tower for a generous sum and to light it with modern lamps at his own expense.

The council was inclined to agree. Then the tide turned, and Sister Maren spoke.

"The charter of the tower," she said, "grants it to the Lamplighters' Guild for as long as the Guild has a living member and the lantern burns on the night of the Drowned Bell. That is nine nights from now. If the lantern burns, the tower stays with the Guild. If it does not, the council may do as it likes."

Then she said nothing more, because the tide had finished turning.

Corwin Dask smiled politely and thanked her for the history lesson. Ilsa Varn, who had only enough tidesalt left for one more night, did not smile at all.

Chapter Three: Beneath the Kelp Moors

The tidesalt caves lay under the Kelp Moors, an hour's walk north along the cliffs, and the only way in was through the sea door at low tide. The Guild had once kept a map of the caves in the Archive. When Sister Maren went to find it, the drawer was empty.

So Ilsa went without a map. Tobin went with her, because he refused to be left behind, and because he knew the moors better than anyone: he had spent half his childhood hunting gull eggs there. They took rope, a lamp, Hesper's tin lantern for a spare, and three days of bread.

The sea door was a crack in the cliff that only showed when the water dropped. Inside, the walls glittered. The first chamber should have been thick with tidesalt, crystals as long as a man's arm growing from the ceiling like frozen rain. It had been stripped bare. Fresh pick marks scarred the stone, and a trail of blue dust led deeper into the dark.

They followed it for hours. They passed a chamber where the floor dropped away into black water, and another where the walls hummed when the tide pushed against the rock outside. Twice Tobin heard voices ahead and they hid behind fallen stone while Syndicate miners passed, dragging sledges loaded with crystal. The miners wore red threads around their wrists and spoke of a deadline. Everything had to be out before the night of the Drowned Bell, they said. Dask wanted the caves empty, so that the Guild would have nothing left to burn.

In the deepest chamber they found the heart of the caves: a single crystal the size of a rowing boat, glowing faintly on its own, untouched because it was too big to move. The miners had been trying to break it. There were wedges driven into one side and a cracked sledgehammer lying on the floor.

Ilsa put her hand on the crystal and closed her eyes. Her grandmother had told her about this place, she said. The Mother Salt. Every crystal in the caves grew from it, the way mushrooms grow from a root beneath the soil. If the Syndicate broke it, the caves would never grow again, and there would never be another light on the Drowned Bell Tower.

They could not carry the Mother Salt. But a single shard, chipped from the cracked side where the miners had already done the damage, would burn for a month.

Behind them, a lamp came around the corner. Corwin Dask stood in the tunnel mouth in his grey oilskin coat, with four miners at his back, and for once he was not smiling.

"Mistress Varn," he said. "You are a very long way from your tower. And the tide is coming in."

He was right. Far behind them, they could hear the sea door beginning to roar as the water rose. In an hour the caves would flood to the ceiling, and anyone still inside would stay there until the next low tide, if they were still alive to see it.

Ilsa Varn looked at the shard in her hand, and at the boy beside her, and at the man blocking the only way out. The night of the Drowned Bell was eight days away.

What she did next is for you to decide.
